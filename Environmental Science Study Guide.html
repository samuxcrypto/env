Environmental Science Study Guide

10 key questions with core answers and course examples. Tap a question to expand.

Q1.What is the “environment,” and which fields make up environmental science?
  • Environment: the surroundings or conditions, both living (biotic) and nonliving (abiotic), in which an organism exists.
  • Environmental science: an interdisciplinary field studying the natural world and humanity’s relationship with it.

Collaborating fields

  1. Natural and applied sciences: ecology, geology, chemistry, engineering.
  2. Social sciences: anthropology, psychology, economics.
  3. Humanities: art, literature, music, ethics.

Course examples

  • Empirical science: Greenland glaciologists measure glacier melt rates.
  • Applied science: engineers design hydroelectric systems using glacial meltwater.
  • Social sciences/humanities: researchers study Viking settlement patterns, soil management, and cultural attachment to place.
Q2.What are some environmental dilemmas, and why are they “wicked problems”?
  • Dilemmas: fossil-fuel climate change, population growth (over 9 billion by 2050), pollution, deforestation, topsoil degradation.
  • Wicked problems: multiple interconnected causes and consequences; every solution involves trade-offs.
  • Triple bottom line: weigh environmental, economic, and social causes and consequences together.

Course examples

  • Trade-offs: renewables cut pollution but cost more upfront; reforestation captures CO₂ but uses farmland.

Diamond’s 5 factors of collapse (Greenland Vikings)

  1. Natural climate change (Little Ice Age cooling).
  2. Self-inflicted damage (overgrazing, topsoil erosion).
  3. Failure to adapt (refused to eat local cod).
  4. Hostile neighbors (conflict with the Inuit blocked learning seal hunting).
  5. Loss of friendly trade (ships stopped bringing iron and timber).
Q3.What does sustainable mean, and what do sustainable ecosystems look like?

Sustainably means living within the environment’s means without reducing its ability to support life in the future.

Four characteristics

  1. Renewable energy: continuous, easily replenished inputs.
  2. Sustainable matter use: one organism’s waste is another’s resource.
  3. Population control: populations stay within carrying capacity.
  4. Local biodiversity: improves energy capture, recycling, and resilience.

Course examples

  • Energy: plants turn sunlight into glucose, powering the food chain.
  • Matter: plants absorb CO₂; deer eat plants; wolves eat deer; decomposers return carbon to soil and air.
  • Population control: wolves keep deer from overgrazing.
Q4.What can societies and individuals do to pursue sustainability?

Biomimicry: emulating nature’s models, mentorship, and metrics in agriculture, industry, and energy.

  1. Renewable energy: move from fossil fuels to solar, wind, geothermal, sustainable biomass.
  2. Sustainable matter use: reduce consumption and toxic emissions; reuse and recycle.
  3. Control population growth: voluntary social and educational programs that lower birth rates without raising death rates.
  4. Leverage diversity: protect ecosystems, use regional materials, draw on diverse community ideas.

Course examples

  • Energy: solar and wind replacing coal mimics photosynthetic energy capture.
  • Icelandic settlers: after clearing 80% of forests, they switched from beef to cod, saved wood, and abandoned fragile highlands, so their society survived.
Q5.What challenges does humanity face, and how does environmental literacy help?
  1. Social traps: choices with short-term benefits but long-term harm to society.
  2. Wealth inequalities: the wealthiest 20% control about 80% of resources and can temporarily insulate themselves; the poor extract resources to survive.
  3. Conflicting worldviews: differing ethics shape how people value nature.

Environmental literacy gives people the knowledge to recognize trade-offs and social traps and to turn values into effective legislation.

Course examples

  • Tragedy of the commons: farmers overstock an unowned pasture until it is ruined.
  • Time delay: giant fishing nets give big catches now, then fishery collapse.
  • Sliding reinforcer: pesticides kill pests, then select for resistant ones.
  • Inequality: wealthy nations’ demand for mahogany drives deforestation, while poor landowners cut trees to feed families.
  • Worldviews: anthropocentric (only humans have intrinsic value), biocentric (all living things), ecocentric (intact ecosystems and nutrient cycles).
Q6.How do scientists study nature, and why is science a “process”?
  • Method: gather empirical evidence (from observation or instruments) to draw inferences and test hypotheses.
  • Process: science is both knowledge and the ongoing way of acquiring it; explanations evolve with new evidence.
  • Open to study: science doesn’t require absolute proof; certainty grows as independent lines of evidence accumulate.

Course examples

  • Field research: the Finnish Ultraviolet International Research Center tests UV-B effects on plant growth and DNA damage.
  • Process: the CFC–ozone correlation was refined by lab experiments and Antarctic sampling into cause and effect.
Q7.What is stratospheric ozone, and why does its depletion worry scientists?

A layer of ozone (O₃) in the stratosphere that shields Earth from solar ultraviolet (UV) radiation.

Three forms of UV

  1. UV-C: most energetic; 100% absorbed by O₂ in the stratosphere.
  2. UV-B: damages DNA; ozone absorbs about 90%, so 10% reaches the surface.
  3. UV-A: least energetic; about 50% reaches the surface, causing sunburn and skin aging.

Concern: thinning lets more UV-B through, raising skin cancer rates, damaging plants, and threatening marine food webs.

Course examples

  • Biology: more UV-B harms crops and causes DNA mutations in skin cells.
  • Health: in Southern Chile, skin cancer rose sharply when the Antarctic ozone hole expanded over the region.
Q8.What evidence suggests CFCs deplete stratospheric ozone?

CFCs: synthetic carbon–fluorine–chlorine compounds from the 1930s, used in refrigerators, air conditioners, aerosols, and foam containers.

  1. Observation: at Halley Bay, Antarctica, ozone fell as CFC-12 rose (negative correlation).
  2. Lab and theory: UV-B breaks CFCs apart, releasing chlorine atoms that catalytically convert O₃ to O₂ in a chain reaction.
  3. Field evidence: Susan Solomon’s team sampled stratospheric air at McMurdo Station in spring 1986–1987.

Course example

  • ClO detection: they found high chlorine monoxide just when ozone dropped. ClO forms when chlorine reacts with ozone, so it was direct proof that CFC-derived chlorine was destroying ozone.
Q9.Why are both observational and experimental studies needed, and how does statistics help?
  • Observational: collect data in real settings without manipulating variables; needed for complex systems or when manipulation is unethical or impossible.
  • Experimental: manipulate an independent variable in a test group, measure the dependent variable, and compare to a control group.
  • Statistics: tests whether differences between groups are significant rather than random chance.

Course examples

  • Observational (Abarca et al.): tracked skin cancer in Southern Chile, 1987–1993 vs. 1994–2000, near the ozone hole.
  • Experimental (Menzies et al.): mice exposed to extra UV-B reached 100% skin cancer vs. 0% in controls, showing cause and effect.
Q10.How do the precautionary principle and adaptive management help policy makers?
  • Policy: a formal plan for a desired outcome, “translating our values into action.”
  • Precautionary principle: act to keep a margin of safety when serious harm is threatened, even if the extent is scientifically uncertain.
  • Adaptive management: periodically review and adjust policy as new evidence and monitoring data arrive.

Course examples

  • Precaution: the 1987 Montreal Protocol set CFC phase-out deadlines before all consequences were known.
  • Adaptation: amendments (London 1990, Copenhagen 1992, Beijing 1999) sped up phase-outs and added new ozone-depleting substances, driving down chlorine emissions.
